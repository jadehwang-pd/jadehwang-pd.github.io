06After launch

After launch, users told us “good” meant two different things.

We launched with one global setting. Then reviews and support messages split in two directions: users in Europe and North America said the results no longer looked like them, while users in Asia asked to look a little prettier. Instead of picking one side, I set different generation values per region: true likeness for Western markets, slight idealization for Asian markets. Same templates, same three-tap flow, a different definition of “good” for each audience.

08Learnings

The best AI experience is the one users don’t notice.

01
Absorb complexity, don’t hand it overOur users weren’t AI users. They had never written a prompt and didn’t want to learn. So the right move wasn’t a better prompt editor, it was hiding the AI entirely and letting them just pick the photo they wanted.
02
Ship, then listenWe couldn’t have predicted that “a good photo” meant different things by market. Post-launch feedback, not assumptions, told us how to tune it, so the work didn’t end at launch.
09If I built it today

Same core bet. Sharper focus.

Templates over prompts still holds. What’s changed is the technology around it — and what I now know about launching with zero users.

01Buy quality, own the taste
THENSelf-hosted Stable Diffusion, quality held up by 1,700+ hand-tuned prompts.
NOWGenerate with the latest image models via API (e.g. OpenAI), still delivered as templates.
Model quality is now a commodity anyone can rent. The moat is the curated template library and knowing what users want to look like.
02From photos to video
THENStill photos only.
NOWExtend each template into short video — moving portraits ready for Reels and Shorts.
Same face data, same three-tap flow, more value per template. And it follows where self-expression on social media has already moved.
03Single-player first
THENProfiles, following and followers built in from day one.
NOWA solo tool: make, save and share out to the networks users already have.
A social feed with no users is an empty room. Earn the habit first; add community only once there’s density to support it.

06 · DECISIONS

Three choices that make an AI call feel safe.

A shorter flow wasn’t enough on its own. People still had to trust the AI enough to hand over the call, so each decision answers one of the trust questions above.

Key design decisions.See it, steer it, trust it.
01Trust: the AI says who it is
ChoseThe AI introduces itself as NAVER’s assistant calling for a customer, before anything else.
OverSounding human and hiding that it’s an AI.
Answers the store-owner question from review: an unknown voice sounds like spam. Being upfront keeps the call going and protects NAVER’s brand.
02Control: watch and step in
ChoseA live transcript with suggested reply chips, so the user can jump in at any moment.
OverA result-only flow where the user waits for a summary.
Handing a call to AI feels risky. In the second survey, users wanted to see how the call went, so the live call lets them check and step in at any moment.
03Failure: plan for the missed call
ChoseAuto-dial at opening time, retry up to 3 times, then suggest prioritized alternatives.
OverFailing with an error and asking the user to try again.
A failed call is the most common outcome with small stores. The user should never have to start over.

Epilogue.

What I took away.

01
A prototype changes the conversation.Until people could see the call happen on screen, the idea stayed abstract. The first clickable prototype is what turned “why an AI call?” into “great. just keep going.”
02
Design for the moment people lose control.Letting AI speak for you only works if you can take the wheel back. Changing the live call from view-only to step-in was the decision that made the whole idea feel safe.
03
A “no” is a question, not a verdict.“Users won’t trust an AI to call for them” sounded like rejection. It was really a request for evidence. Once I answered it, the idea that nearly died at the second review became the one an executive wanted to use.

Case study 03 · Wonderful

A diary about your feelings should feel like one.

Wonderful is a self-esteem diary: log your mood, answer one question a day, take a small action for yourself. People liked the idea, but not the place they were writing it in.

RoleProduct designer
TeamPM · Frontend · Backend · Me
PeriodNov – Dec 2021
ScopeFull UI redesign
01 · The problem

People were writing about their feelings in something that looked like a dashboard.

Reviews and user feedback kept saying the same thing in different words: the app felt businesslike. Big saturated blocks, mood as text labels, every card shouting at the same volume. Nothing invited you to slow down and write. And people weren’t staying.

★★☆☆☆ · REVIEW“It feels like a work app, not a diary.”
SUPPORT“The design is too stiff. I don’t feel like writing here.”
★★★☆☆ · REVIEW“Love the idea. Wish it looked softer.”
Paraphrased from app reviews and user feedback.
3-MONTH RETENTION · BEFORE 14% Fewer than 1 in 7 users were still writing three months later.
02 · The insight

The features weren’t the problem. The tone was.

The daily question, the action and the story all worked. What didn’t work was how they were presented. A diary is private and emotional, so the redesign had one goal: keep every feature, and change how it feels to use them. The PM agreed to a UI-only release, so the developers didn’t have to touch the data model.

03 · What changed

Same screen, five decisions.

BEFORE
AFTER
01Mood
BEFOREWords in outlined circles: sad, happy, xoxo.
AFTERHand-drawn faces you recognize before you read them.
02Color
BEFOREFive saturated blocks competing for attention.
AFTEROne warm, muted palette. Brighter color only for moods.
03Texture
BEFOREFlat panels, like a settings screen.
AFTERA photo, a paper strip and tape labels, like a real journal.
04Hierarchy
BEFOREQuestion, Action and Story as three equally heavy cards.
AFTERToday’s question in focus; the three entries as soft, overlapping circles.
05History
BEFOREPast days as letters: S, H, X.
AFTERPast days as the faces you picked, on a strip of paper.

People started reviewing the design, not just the idea.

05 · Looking back

Tone is a feature.

In an emotional product, how a screen feels is part of what it does. If I did this today, I’d ship the redesign to a share of users first. Retention also moves for reasons outside the UI, and a split test would have shown exactly how much of the jump came from the design.

Case study 04 · Gongpal

What if you didn’t need a streak
— just someone to talk to?

A language app where you learn a new language by texting AI friends who remember you. It started with a problem I lived through.

RoleProduct designer (100%)
TeamMe + 1 developer
PeriodOct 2026
ToolsFigma · Claude Design · ChatGPT
01 · Where it started

I moved to the US in middle school.
Every language app I tried, I quit.

Streaks kept me going a few extra days, never more. What I actually wanted was a friend who was there just for me — someone I could talk to whenever, in the language I was learning.

“Make a friend who only speaks English. You’ll pick it up fast.” — what everyone around me kept saying

They were half joking, but they were right. With someone you care about, you don’t think about lessons — you just need the language to reach them. Affection is the motivation. So could that be turned into a product?

02 · The question

Why does almost every language app have a streak?

Because the hardest part of learning a language isn’t the language. It’s staying motivated long enough to keep using it. So apps build external reasons to come back.

EXTERNAL REASON“I have to study.”Streaks · daily goals · XP · reminders. Works for days, fades in weeks.
INTERNAL REASON“I want to talk to them.”A friend who remembers you and has a life of their own. The reason to return is the relationship.
03 · The signal

People are already hacking Character.AI to learn languages.

I wasn’t the only one. One user-made tutor bot alone has 33.5K chats. Learners want this — they just have to build it themselves, on a product that wasn’t made for it.

But it isn’t built for learning.
  • Setup is on you: no level, no corrections unless you prompt for them
  • Nothing is kept: no saved phrases, no review
  • Characters drift, so the relationship can feel unreliable
04 · Where learners drop off

Learners had to pick: study, or feel connected.

Most AI chat in language apps is still a tutor in disguise: it steers you back to the lesson, grades every reply and ends when the exercise does. That’s the moment people close the app. Gongpal is a friend, not a tutor — the conversation goes wherever you take it.

05 · Design principle

It should never feel like AI.

If the relationship is the product, the friend has to feel real. So the whole interface removes every signal that says “this is a bot”: no AI badges, no sparkle icons, no instant answers. You should feel like you’re texting a person.

01Real faces
INSTEAD OF3D mascots, illustrated bots and “AI” badges.
GONGPALFriends look like real people: candid photos at work, on the street, in a café.
02Human timing
INSTEAD OFAnswers that appear the instant you hit send.
GONGPALThe friend reads, types, pauses, and often replies in two messages. It takes a few seconds, like a person.
03Real speech
INSTEAD OFTextbook sentences and perfect formality.
GONGPALCasual speech, slang and laughs, the way a native-speaking friend actually texts.
04Quiet learning
INSTEAD OFScores and red marks that stop the chat.
GONGPALOne line slips under your message after the friend replies. The chat never stops.
06 · Try it

Text Daniel. He takes a moment to reply.

This is the live prototype. Open a friend, send a message and watch a new language pick up through everyday chat.

Demo shown in Spanish.
07 · Trade-offs

Every call came back to one question: does this make the friend feel more real?

01No streaks, no XP, no leaderboards.

Every competitor uses them, and they do lift short-term return. But they build an external reason that fades, and they make the friend feel like a game character.

CHOSEPull users back with friends who text first and remember your day.
GAVE UPA proven short-term retention lever.
02Open conversation over scripted roleplay.

Scenarios are easy to build and measure. But a script reminds you it’s a lesson. Real-world practice still matters, so it lives in a separate Scenes mode.

CHOSEFree-flowing chats with friends; one-off Scenes (café, taxi) with goals and a review.
GAVE UPA tightly structured curriculum in the main chat.
08 · Iteration

Three things that made it feel like a bot.

Each early version worked, but somewhere it gave away that you were talking to a machine. So each one was redesigned around the principle.

01 · FIRST VERSIONReplies arrived the instant you hit send.Fast, but no person texts back in 0.3 seconds.
NOWRead receipt, typing, a pause, then two messages. A reply takes a few seconds.
02 · FIRST VERSIONCorrections came as a graded card.The chat turned into a quiz and stopped there.
NOWOne line under your own message, shown only after the friend replies.
03 · FIRST VERSIONFriends were illustrated avatars.They read as “an AI product” before the first message.
NOWReal-photo style friends with a camera-roll feel.

You stay for the friend.
